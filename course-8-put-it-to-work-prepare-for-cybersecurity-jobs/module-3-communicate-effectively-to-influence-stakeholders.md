# Module 3: Communicate Effectively to Influence Stakeholders

## Security Stakeholders

A **stakeholder** is an individual or group with an interest in an organization's decisions or activities. A large part of an analyst's job is reporting findings to security stakeholders at various levels.

| Stakeholder | Role | Direct contact as entry-level analyst? |
|---|---|---|
| **Cybersecurity risk manager** | Leads efforts to identify, assess, and mitigate security risks | Yes — regular |
| **Operations manager** | Oversees day-to-day security operations; leads teams developing and implementing security strategies against cyber threats | Yes — regular |
| **CEO** (Chief Executive Officer) | Highest-ranking person in the organization | Unlikely |
| **CFO** (Chief Financial Officer) | High-level financial stakeholder | Unlikely |
| **CISO** (Chief Information Security Officer) | Highest-level security stakeholder | Unlikely |

### Communicating with Operations and Risk Managers

When reporting to these stakeholders, clearly state the **current security issue and its possible causes**. The operations manager then decides next steps and coordinates others to resolve it.

> Example: you report repeated failed logins on an employee's account. The operations manager may contact the employee's supervisor to determine whether it's a genuine wrong-password problem or a compromised account, and they may discuss how account lockouts could affect day-to-day operations. You might then help implement preventative measures once next steps are decided.

### How Information Flows Upward

Operations and risk managers rely on analysts to keep them informed, and they in turn report to the CISO and CFO to give a broader picture of the organization's security posture. Even without talking to senior leaders directly, your work reaches them through your team.

## Clear and Concise Communication

Security messages often contain sensitive details, so be deliberate about **what is shared and with whom**. Good communication is:

- **Clear, concise, and direct** — avoid unnecessary technical jargon so busy stakeholders grasp the impact and purpose immediately
- **Tailored to the audience** — check with your manager or supervisor about what information a stakeholder needs for their role, and share only the most relevant insights
- **Timely and targeted** — helps stakeholders do their jobs and reinforces your value on the team

### Structure Your Message Like a Story

1. **Beginning** — state the specific security problem (e.g., potential malicious code execution spotted while monitoring system logs)
2. **Middle** — explain the organizational impact, and tie the issue to the incident response playbook to show you followed established procedures
3. **End** — propose a potential resolution

Support the narrative with relevant data (key findings reports, lists of issues needing immediate attention). Deliver it through an appropriate channel — email, documents, or incident management/ticketing systems aligned with the security playbooks — and use **visuals** (charts, graphs, dashboards) to help stakeholders make informed decisions.

### Get to the Point

Stakeholders are time-pressed, so ask yourself before sending:

1. What do I want this person to know?
2. Why is it important for them to know it?
3. When do they need to take action?
4. How do I explain this in a non-technical way?

### Follow the Protocols

When joining a team, learn which applications and forms of communication are acceptable for stakeholder communication — in-person meetings, video conferencing, email, or company chat — **before** you start communicating.

## Communicate with Impact

Different stakeholders care about different things:

| Stakeholder Level | Focus | Tailor Your Message To |
|---|---|---|
| **Operations / risk managers** (entry-level analysts talk to these most) | Day-to-day operations, e.g., logging | Information tied to their daily responsibilities, such as anomalies in logs you're escalating |
| **Senior-level stakeholders** | Underlying risks, e.g., the potential financial burden of an incident | The business impact rather than log details |

### Choosing a Communication Method

| Situation | Best Method |
|---|---|
| Straightforward message | Instant message or phone call |
| Complex, multi-layered situation | Email or in-person meeting |
| Lots of data and numbers | A graph, spreadsheet, or slideshow |

Other available methods include video calls, shared spreadsheets, and slideshow presentations — pick whichever fits the situation.

## Using Visuals to Communicate

Stakeholders are often busy with other responsibilities, so showing important information visually helps gain their input and support. Visuals give decision-makers actionable information about risks to the organization's security posture.

### Visual Dashboards

A **visual dashboard** displays various types of data in one place, quickly. They're especially useful for telling stories about security events that involve numbers and data.

- Can be **simple** (a single chart) or **complex** (multiple charts, graphs, and tables) — choose based on the situation and the story you're telling
- Accuracy and attention to detail are essential whenever presenting data to stakeholders
- Tools for building them include **Google Sheets** and **Apache OpenOffice**

### When to Use Visual Communication

Not every update needs a visual — sometimes a simple email, an email with a document attachment, or a quick phone call (which avoids waiting for a reply) is best. But visuals shine when data tells the story.

> Example: your supervisor asks for results of an internal audit counting how many phishing emails each of five departments clicked over five months. Rather than describing the numbers in an email, a graph or chart shows the findings at a glance and is easier for the stakeholder to understand quickly.
