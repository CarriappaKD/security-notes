# Module 3: Protect Against Threats, Risks, and Vulnerabilities

## Security Frameworks and Controls

**Security frameworks** are guidelines for building plans to mitigate risks and threats to data and privacy — providing a structured approach to implementing a security lifecycle.

Purpose of security frameworks:
- Protecting personally identifiable information (PII)
- Securing financial information
- Identifying security weaknesses
- Managing organizational risks
- Aligning security with business goals

### Core Components of Security Frameworks

1. **Identifying and documenting security goals** — setting clear objectives, such as aligning with regulations
2. **Setting guidelines to achieve security goals** — developing new policies and procedures to meet compliance requirements
3. **Implementing strong security processes** — designing and executing procedures to ensure compliance
4. **Monitoring and communicating results** — continuously monitoring for potential security issues and reporting them

Organizations choose a framework by evaluating their **industry regulations, current security maturity, business goals, and risk tolerance**.

> **Security controls** are safeguards designed to reduce specific security risks. They work alongside frameworks to ensure security goals and processes are implemented correctly and that the organization meets regulatory compliance requirements.
>
> **Compliance** is the process of adhering to internal standards and external regulations.

## Secure Design — The CIA Triad

The three foundational principles used by security professionals to establish controls that mitigate threats, risks, and vulnerabilities:

- **Confidentiality** — only authorized individuals can access specific assets/data, enforced through strict access controls
- **Integrity** — data is accurate, authentic, and reliable; methods like encryption prevent tampering
- **Availability** — authorized users can access data whenever needed

**Assets**: anything an organization considers valuable. Assets storing sensitive data (e.g., applications handling customer records) require far stricter security controls than lower-sensitivity assets.

## Ethics in Cybersecurity

Security professionals have significant access to data and systems, which makes unbiased, ethical conduct essential. Ethical guidelines help guide appropriate decision-making.

### Key Ethical Principles

- **Confidentiality (ethics)** — safeguarding proprietary or private information; avoiding unauthorized sharing
- **Privacy protections** — protecting personal information from unauthorized use by following company policy and procedure, even under pressure from urgent requests by superiors
- **Laws** — violating regulations (e.g., mishandling confidential data) can lead to serious legal consequences

### Ethical Concerns Around Counterattacks

Deploying a counterattack against a threat actor is **illegal** — it's considered an act of vigilantism. Security professionals can only **defend**, not retaliate. A counterattack can also cause further escalation of the original attack.
